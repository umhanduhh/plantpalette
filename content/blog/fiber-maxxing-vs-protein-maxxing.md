For the last few years, "protein-maxxing" was the nutrition trend. Protein powder in the coffee, protein added to everything from pasta to popcorn, and a running mental tally of grams by 6pm. Walk into any grocery store and the shelves still show it — protein-fortified everything, positioned front and center.

But going into 2026, the conversation is shifting. Nutrition researchers, including experts at Johns Hopkins' Center for a Livable Future, are pointing out something the protein trend quietly ignored: **most Americans already get plenty of protein. Almost none of us get enough fiber.**

That gap is what "fiber-maxxing" is trying to close.

## What Is Fiber-Maxxing?

Fiber-maxxing is the practice of deliberately prioritizing high-fiber foods at every meal — not as a diet restriction, but as the main event. Instead of asking "where's my protein this meal," the question becomes "where's my fiber."

It's less a diet and more a correction. The Dietary Guidelines recommend **25–38 grams of fiber per day**, and most adults land closer to 15. That gap has real consequences: fiber is directly tied to digestive health, blood sugar regulation, cholesterol, and — increasingly — gut microbiome diversity, which researchers now connect to immunity and even mood.

## Why Protein-Maxxing Left This Gap Open

Protein-maxxing isn't wrong, exactly — it's just incomplete. Chasing a protein number can quietly crowd out fiber-rich foods, especially when "protein" gets fulfilled through powders, bars, and animal products rather than legumes, whole grains, or vegetables — the foods that carry both.

The nutrition trend cycle tends to treat macronutrients like competing teams, but fiber and protein aren't actually in conflict. Lentils, chickpeas, edamame, and oats deliver meaningful amounts of both. The shift toward fiber-maxxing isn't anti-protein — it's a nudge back toward foods that were getting skipped.

## How to Fiber-Max Without Counting Every Gram

Counting grams works for some people, but it's not the only way in. A few lower-friction approaches:

- **Lead with a plant at every meal.** Before you plan the protein, pick the vegetable, legume, or whole grain first.
- **Upgrade your grain.** Swapping white rice for farro, quinoa, or barley adds several grams of fiber with zero extra effort.
- **Keep the skin on.** Apples, potatoes, and cucumbers lose a meaningful chunk of fiber when peeled.
- **Make legumes a default, not a special occasion.** A can of black beans or chickpeas added to almost anything — soup, salad, grain bowls — is one of the fastest fiber wins available.
- **Snack on whole fruit over juice.** Juicing removes most of the fiber that made the fruit filling in the first place.

## FAQ

**How much fiber should I eat per day?**

The general guideline is 25 grams per day for women and 38 grams for men, though needs vary by age and individual health factors.

**Can you eat too much fiber?**

Yes — increasing fiber too quickly can cause bloating or digestive discomfort. Most nutrition guidance recommends increasing intake gradually and drinking enough water alongside it.

**Is fiber-maxxing a real diet?**

Not in the structured sense — there's no meal plan or elimination involved. It's better understood as a shift in emphasis: prioritizing fiber-rich foods rather than treating them as an afterthought to protein or calories.

**Do fiber supplements count the same as food?**

Supplements can help close a gap, but whole foods bring along additional benefits — like plant diversity and micronutrients — that isolated fiber supplements don't replicate.
