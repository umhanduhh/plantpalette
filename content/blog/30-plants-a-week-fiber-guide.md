If you've seen "fiber-maxxing" all over your feed lately, you're not imagining it — 2026 is the year fiber finally got its moment after a few years of protein dominating every headline. And the number one rule behind it isn't about eating less. It's about eating more variety.

That's where the "30 plants a week" goal comes in.

## What Does "30 Plants a Week" Actually Mean?

The idea is simple: instead of counting grams of fiber or calories, you count different plant foods — fruits, vegetables, whole grains, legumes, nuts, seeds, herbs, and spices — you eat across seven days. The goal is 30 unique plants, not 30 servings.

The number comes from gut-health research (popularized by gastroenterologists like Dr. Will Bulsiewicz) showing that a diverse gut microbiome — one that's been fed a wide range of plant fibers — is linked to better digestion, immunity, and even mood. A microbiome that only ever sees broccoli and bananas doesn't get the same benefit as one that sees broccoli, black beans, oats, walnuts, thyme, and blueberries in the same week.

Why this beats counting grams of fiber:

- It's visual and intuitive instead of math-heavy
- It naturally pushes you toward whole foods over fiber supplements
- It rewards variety, which is what your gut microbiome actually responds to
- Herbs, spices, and even coffee count — so it adds up faster than you'd think

## Fiber-Maxxing vs. Protein-Maxxing

For the last few years, "protein-maxxing" dominated nutrition trends — hit your protein number, and everything else was secondary. But most people in the U.S. already get enough protein. What they're missing is fiber: the recommended intake is 25–38 grams a day, and most adults get less than half that.

Fiber-maxxing is the correction. It's not anti-protein — it's "stop ignoring the other macronutrient that's actually undersupplied." And plant diversity is the easiest on-ramp, because you don't need to calculate anything. You just need to notice what's already on your plate.

## How to Count Your 30 Plants (Without a Spreadsheet)

A few ground rules that make this realistic instead of overwhelming:

1. Herbs and spices count individually. Basil, cumin, and garlic are three separate plants, not one "seasoning."
2. Different varieties count separately — a red bell pepper and a green bell pepper are two different plants. So are white and sweet potatoes.
3. Whole grains and legumes count. Oats, quinoa, lentils, and chickpeas all add to your total — and they carry serious fiber weight.
4. Frozen and canned count. Frozen peas are still peas. Canned black beans are still black beans.
5. Coffee and tea count. Yes, really — they're plant-derived.

A realistic week might look like: oats, blueberries, walnuts, spinach, garlic, chickpeas, cumin, brown rice, broccoli, red onion, lime, cilantro, black beans, avocado, corn, bell pepper, tomato, basil — and you're already past 15 without trying hard.

## Simple Ways to Add Plant Variety This Week

- Swap one grain for a new one (try farro or barley instead of rice)
- Add a handful of a new herb to something you already cook often
- Pick a vegetable you've never bought before at the store
- Add a spoonful of a new seed (chia, hemp, flax) to breakfast
- Choose a bean or lentil you haven't tried instead of your usual protein

None of these require a new recipe or a Sunday meal-prep session — they're single swaps inside meals you already make. For more low-effort ideas, see [How to Eat More Vegetables Without Meal Prepping Every Sunday](/blog/eat-more-vegetables-without-meal-prep).

## FAQ

**Is 30 plants a week the same as 30 servings?**

No. It's 30 different plant foods across the week, regardless of serving size. A small pinch of oregano counts the same as a full cup of spinach for this purpose.

**Do I need to hit exactly 30?**

No — it's a directional goal, not a hard rule. Most people eating a typical Western diet are closer to 10–15 unique plants a week, so even reaching 20 is a meaningful jump in diversity.

**Does this replace counting fiber grams?**

Not necessarily, but for most people, chasing variety naturally increases total fiber intake without the friction of tracking every gram.

**What's the easiest way to actually track this?**

A running note on your phone works, but a tool that recognizes food from a photo (like Plate Palette) removes the manual list-making entirely.
