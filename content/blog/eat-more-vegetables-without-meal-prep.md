"Eat more vegetables" is the most repeated piece of nutrition advice there is — and also the least specific. Most of the guidance that follows assumes you have a free Sunday afternoon, a stack of glass containers, and the motivation to chop five pounds of produce at once. If that's not realistic for your week, meal prep isn't the only path in.

Here's a lower-friction version: instead of overhauling how you cook, you add vegetables into the meals and habits you already have.

## Why Meal Prep Isn't the Only Answer

Meal prep works well for some people and fails quietly for everyone else — the prepped vegetables go soggy in the fridge, the Sunday session gets skipped one week and never comes back, or the novelty wears off by week three. None of that means you're bad at this. It means the system didn't fit.

Vegetable intake goes up more reliably through small, repeatable habits than through one big weekly effort. A five-minute addition you actually keep doing beats a two-hour prep session you do twice and abandon.

## Low-Effort Ways to Add Vegetables

- Add one vegetable to whatever you're already making. Frozen spinach into pasta sauce, shredded carrots into rice, chopped bell pepper into eggs — these add minutes, not meal plans.
- Keep a "grab and eat" vegetable in sight. Cherry tomatoes, snap peas, or baby carrots on the counter get eaten simply because they're visible and require zero prep.
- Buy pre-cut when it removes the barrier. Pre-washed greens, pre-cut squash, and bagged slaw exist for a reason — paying slightly more for zero chopping is a legitimate strategy, not cheating.
- Use frozen vegetables as a default, not a backup. Frozen vegetables are flash-frozen at peak ripeness and retain nutrients well; they also never go bad in the crisper drawer before you get to them.
- Add vegetables to sauces and bases instead of as a side. A pasta sauce or curry base is an easy place to hide extra volume without adding a separate dish to cook.
- Make one meal a week vegetable-forward by default. Not a full overhaul — just designate something like "soup night" or "stir-fry night" where vegetables are naturally the main ingredient.

## Reframe the Goal: Variety Over Volume

Most advice focuses on quantity — cups per day, servings per meal. But variety matters just as much for both nutrition and staying interested in eating vegetables at all. Rotating between a handful of different vegetables, rather than eating the same broccoli every night, keeps things from feeling like a chore and naturally covers a wider range of nutrients.

If you want a concrete target for that variety, the **[30 plants a week](/blog/30-plants-a-week-fiber-guide)** goal is a good one to borrow — it turns "eat a wider variety" into something you can actually count.

## A Simple Starting Habit

If you only take one thing from this: pick a single meal you eat most days (breakfast, lunch, whatever's most consistent) and commit to adding one vegetable to it. Once that's automatic, add a second habit. This compounds faster than a single ambitious overhaul, and it survives busy weeks — which is the actual test.

## FAQ

**Do frozen vegetables count the same as fresh?**

Yes. Frozen vegetables are typically frozen shortly after harvest and retain comparable — sometimes higher — nutrient levels than fresh produce that's spent days in transit and storage.

**How many vegetables should I actually be eating?**

General guidance suggests 2–3 cups of vegetables a day for most adults, but consistency and variety matter more than hitting an exact number every single day.

**What if I don't like most vegetables?**

Start with format, not variety — roasting, or adding vegetables into sauces and grain bowls, changes texture and flavor significantly compared to eating them plain or steamed.

**Is buying pre-cut vegetables worth the extra cost?**

If the extra cost is what keeps you eating vegetables consistently, it's a reasonable trade — the health benefit of actually eating them outweighs the modest premium.
